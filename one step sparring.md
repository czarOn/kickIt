One-Step Sparring in Taekwondo: How It Works, With Examples
TaeKwonDo One Step Sparring
One-step sparring is a taekwondo partner drill in which one student makes a single, announced attack, usually a step-in punch, and the other answers with a set block and counterattack that stops just short of contact. It teaches timing, distance, accuracy and control before students move on to free sparring, and it is part of most color belt tests. ITF schools call it ilbo matsogi; Kukkiwon and World Taekwondo schools call it hanbeon gyeorugi, one of several kinds of pre-arranged “promise” sparring. Each school has its own list of one-steps, and students add new ones as they rise in rank.

How it works

How to do one-step sparring
The exact routine differs between schools, but most follow this pattern.

Face your partner at a distance where a full step forward with a punch would just reach you, then bow.
The attacker gets ready, often by stepping back into a front stance with a low block, and signals with a kihap.
The defender stands in a ready stance and kihaps back to show they are ready.
The attacker steps forward with the agreed attack, most often a middle or high punch.
The defender blocks or moves off the line, then counters with one or more techniques, stopping short of contact, and often finishes with a kihap.
Both return to the start, bow if the school requires it, and switch roles.
Good one-steps start from solid stances and proper etiquette; see taekwondo stances and bowing in taekwondo.


Tip

The attacker’s job matters too. A committed, accurate punch at the right distance gives the defender something real to work against; a weak or short attack makes the drill pointless.

Examples

Examples of one-step sparring combinations
These are common patterns against a step-in right punch. Your school’s list will set the exact moves.

Block and punch

Inside block, reverse punch
Step back into a front stance, block the punch, then counter with a reverse punch to the body.

Block and kick

Outside block, roundhouse
Block to the outside of the arm, then counter with a roundhouse kick to the body.

Step off the line

Knife-hand block, side kick
Step at an angle with a knife-hand block, then side kick to the ribs.

Close range

Palm block, elbow strike
Redirect the punch with a palm block and step in with an elbow strike.

Combination

Block, kick, punch
Block, front kick, then finish with a punch, keeping balance between each move.

Advanced

Spinning counters
Higher belts add jumping or spinning kicks once the basics are clean.

Types

Types of step sparring, from three-step to free
Type	ITF name	How it works
Three-step	Sambo matsogi	The attacker steps in three times; the defender blocks three times, then counters.
Two-step	Ibo matsogi	Two set attacks, usually mixing hands and feet, then a counter.
One-step	Ilbo matsogi	One attack; the defender blocks, dodges or intercepts and counters.
Semi-free	Ban jayu matsogi	Partners take turns attacking and defending with more freedom of movement.
Free	Jayu matsogi	Any agreed technique to legal targets, with control or protective gear.
Kukkiwon and World Taekwondo schools use the same idea under the name yaksok gyeorugi (promise sparring), with three-step, two-step and one-step versions before free sparring (gyeorugi).


Belt tests

One-step sparring in belt tests
Most schools give each belt a set of one-steps to learn, adding new ones at every rank.
At a test you may be asked to show any of them, on both sides, in any order.
Examiners look for correct distance, strong stances, clean blocks, accurate counters, control and a confident kihap.
Higher belts may face unannounced attacks or counter-and-recounter drills, where the attacker answers your counter.

Practice

How to practice one-step sparring
Learn each one-step slowly, piece by piece: footwork, block, then counter.
Practice both sides so your left and right are equally sharp.
Use shadow sparring at home: picture the punch, then run the block and counter in front of a mirror.
Work with a partner at half speed first, then build to full speed with control.
Mark your stance length with tape on the floor to keep distance consistent.
Before a test, film yourself running your whole list and check stances, hands and kihap.

Mistakes

Common mistakes
Wrong distance: too close and the punch jams; too far and the counter misses.
Moving before the attack: wait for the punch, or the drill teaches nothing.
Dropped hands after the block, leaving you open.
Uncontrolled counters that hit your partner.
A weak kihap and loose stances, which examiners notice right away. See the kihap.


FAQ

Frequently asked questions
What is one-step sparring in taekwondo?
A pre-arranged partner drill in which one student makes a single attack, usually a step-in punch, and the other answers with a set block and counter that stops short of contact.

What is one-step sparring called in Korean?
ITF schools call it ilbo matsogi. Kukkiwon and World Taekwondo schools call it hanbeon gyeorugi, a type of yaksok gyeorugi (promise sparring).

Why do taekwondo schools teach one-step sparring?
It builds timing, distance, control and basic self-defense patterns in a safe way before students try free sparring, and it lets instructors check technique at belt tests.

How many one-steps do you need for a belt test?
It depends on the school. Most assign a few new one-steps at each rank and expect you to know all earlier ones too.

What is the difference between one-step and three-step sparring?
In three-step sparring the attacker steps in three times and the defender blocks three times before countering. One-step sparring has a single attack and a single defense and counter.

Does karate have one-step sparring?
Yes. Karate calls it ippon kumite, and many taekwondo drills share the same pre-arranged format.
